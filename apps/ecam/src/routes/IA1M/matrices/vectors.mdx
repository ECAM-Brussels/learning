# Vectors

We have already seen vectors in the course of Calculus,
where they were used to represent quantities
having both a magnitude and a direction:
positions, displacements, velocities, accelerations and forces.
They were also used to describe geometric objects,
such as the direction of a line or the normal to a plane,
and vector fields, such as the gradient of a function,
which points in the direction of steepest increase.

:::remark

In English, the _direction_ of a vector includes its orientation:
{tex`\mathbf{v}`} and {tex`-\mathbf{v}`} have _opposite directions_.
The French "direction" and "sens" are both covered by this single word.

:::

In all these cases, a vector can be drawn as an arrow,
but it can also be written as an ordered list of numbers: its _components_.
Which point of view is the most useful depends on the context.
In machine learning, the second one is usually the natural choice,
since the components do not need to have a geometric meaning.
For example, a house can be described by its area,
its number of rooms and its age.
These are called _features_ or _attributes_ of the house.

In machine learning, vectors are used to represent data points with {tex`n`} features.
In matrix notation this is written as a column vector:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_1 \\
\vdots \\
x_n
\end{bmatrix}`}

## Basic vector operations

### Transposition

:::definition[Transpose]

Transposing a vector:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}^T=\begin{bmatrix}
x_1 & \cdots & x_n
\end{bmatrix}`}

:::

On its own, transposing a vector does not change any information:
it only turns a column into a row.

In a dataset, each data point is usually written as a row.
For example, a table of houses has one row per house,
and one column per feature:

| House | Area (m²) | Rooms | Age (years) |
| ----- | --------- | ----- | ----------- |
| 1     | 120       | 4     | 15          |
| 2     | 85        | 3     | 40          |

The first house is described by the vector
{tex`\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix} 120 \\ 4 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix}`},
and its row in the table is {tex`\mathbf{x}^T=\begin{bmatrix} 120 & 4 & 15 \end{bmatrix}.`}

Transposing also becomes useful when we compute dot products,
as we will see below.

### Sum and difference

:::definition[Sum and difference]

The _sum_ and _difference_ of two vectors are naturally defined by:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_1+y_1 \\
\vdots \\
x_n+y_n
\end{bmatrix}\qquad\qquad
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_1-y_1 \\
\vdots \\
x_n-y_n
\end{bmatrix}`}

:::

The difference of two vectors {tex`\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}`}
compares two data points feature by feature.
For example, with {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} the two houses of the table:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
=\begin{bmatrix}
120 \\
4 \\
15
\end{bmatrix}
-\begin{bmatrix}
85 \\
3 \\
40
\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}
35 \\
1 \\
-25
\end{bmatrix}`}

The first house is 35 m² larger, has one more room
and is 25 years younger than the second one.

### Dot product

:::definition[Dot product]

The _dot product_ (also called scalar product) of two vectors
{tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} is defined by:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}
=x_1y_1+x_2y_2+\cdots+x_ny_n`}

:::

:::remark

In Calculus, the dot product was written {tex`\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}`}.
Here we write it {tex`\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}`}:
a row multiplied by a column.

{tex`
\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_1 & \cdots & x_n
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
y_1 \\
\vdots \\
y_n
\end{bmatrix}
=x_1y_1+x_2y_2+\cdots+x_ny_n`}

Read it as follows:
go along the row and down the column at the same time,
multiply the components two by two,
and add up the results.
This _row times column_ rule is the basic building block
of the matrix product, which we will see later.

For example, suppose a real estate agent estimates the price of a house
with 3000 € per m², 10000 € per room and −1000 € per year of age.
Writing these prices as a row and the house as a column:

{tex`
\begin{bmatrix}
3000 & 10000 & -1000
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
120 \\
4 \\
15
\end{bmatrix}
=3000\cdot 120+10000\cdot 4-1000\cdot 15
=385000`}

The dot product gives the estimated price: 385000 €.

:::

As a consequence, the _squared norm_ of a vector is:

{tex`
||\mathbf{x}||^2=\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}=x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_n^2`}

The _squared distance_ between two vectors is:

{tex`
||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||^2
=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^T(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})`}

#### Similarity

In Calculus, we have seen the geometric meaning of the dot product:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}=||\mathbf{x}||\,||\mathbf{y}||\cos\theta`}

where {tex`\theta`} is the angle between the two vectors.
This formula remains valid in {tex`n`} dimensions.
The sign of the dot product therefore tells us
whether two vectors point in similar directions:

- if {tex`\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}>0`}, the angle is smaller than 90°,
  and the vectors point in similar directions;
- if {tex`\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}=0`}, the angle is exactly 90°;
- if {tex`\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}<0`}, the angle is larger than 90°,
  and the vectors point in opposite directions.

This makes the dot product a first measure of _similarity_
between two data points.

:::example[Movie ratings]

Four people rate three movies:
{tex`1`} means they liked it, {tex`-1`} that they disliked it,
and {tex`0`} that they have not seen it.

| Person    | Movie 1 | Movie 2 | Movie 3 |
| --------- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| Adam      | 1       | 1       | -1      |
| Batoul    | 1       | 1       | 0       |
| Charlotte | -1      | -1      | 1       |
| Djamel    | 1       | -1      | 0       |

Each person is described by a vector of ratings.
Comparing Adam ({tex`\mathbf{a}`}) to the others:

- {tex`\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}=1+1+0=2`}:
  Adam and Batoul have similar tastes;
- {tex`\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{c}=-1-1-1=-3`}:
  Adam and Charlotte have opposite tastes;
- {tex`\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{d}=1-1+0=0`}:
  Adam and Djamel agree on one movie and disagree on another,
  so their tastes are neither similar nor opposite.

Note that opposite tastes are not the same as unrelated tastes.
Knowing Adam's ratings, we can predict Charlotte's exactly:
they are simply reversed.
Djamel's ratings, on the other hand, tell us nothing about Adam's:
this absence of similarity corresponds to a dot product of zero,
that is to orthogonal vectors.

:::

The dot product is only a _first_ measure of similarity.
Consider the two houses of the table:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}
=\underbrace{120\cdot 85}_{\text{area}}
+\underbrace{4\cdot 3}_{\text{rooms}}
+\underbrace{15\cdot 40}_{\text{age}}
=10200+12+600=10812`}

Almost all of this value comes from the areas:
the number of rooms contributes only 12 out of 10812.
This is not because the number of rooms is less important
to describe a house:
one extra room changes a house much more than one extra m².
It is only because the area is measured in m²,
which gives much larger numbers than a number of rooms.
In other words, the result depends on the _units_ of the features.
To give each feature a fair weight,
the features are usually rescaled to comparable ranges first:
this is called _normalization_, and we will come back to it later.

#### Orthogonality

:::definition[Orthogonality]

Two vectors {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} are _orthogonal_ if

{tex`
\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}=0`}

:::

In 2 or 3 dimensions, orthogonal vectors are perpendicular.
For example, the vectors

{tex`
\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
2
\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
-1
\end{bmatrix}`}

are orthogonal, since {tex`1\cdot 2+2\cdot(-1)=0`}.
In {tex`n`} dimensions, we cannot draw the right angle anymore,
but the dot product still allows us to define it:
in the example above, Adam and Djamel are orthogonal.

{/* that {tex`\mathbf{x}`} is orthogonal to {tex`\mathbf{w}`}: */}
{/* it describes the line or plane */}
{/* whose normal vector is {tex`\mathbf{w}`}. */}

### Scalar multiplication

:::definition[Scalar multiple]

The _scalar multiple_ of a vector {tex`\mathbf{x}`} is

{tex`
\alpha\mathbf{x}`}

where {tex`\alpha`} is a real number.

:::

Geometrically, all scalar multiples of a given vector {tex`\mathbf{x}`}
generate a line through the origin in the direction of {tex`\mathbf{x}`}.

This line is also called the _span_ of a vector {tex`\mathbf{x}`}:

{tex`
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{x})
=\{\alpha\mathbf{x}\:|\:\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}`}

## Linear combinations and span

:::definition[Linear combination]

A _linear combination_ of two vectors is:

{tex`
\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y}`}

where {tex`\alpha`} and {tex`\beta`} are real numbers.

:::

Geometrically, this linear combination generates a vector
lying on the plane defined by {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`},
unless {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} have the same direction.
This plane is called the _span_
of {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`},
that is the set of all possible linear combinations
of {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`}:

{tex`
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
=\{\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y}\:|\:\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\}`}

If {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} are 3-dimensional vectors,
the span is the _subspace_ of {tex`\mathbb{R}^3`}
generated by {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`}.

Drag the heads of {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`}
to see how their span changes
(hold Shift to drag a head vertically):

<SpanOfTwoVectors />

Let's generalize this notion to {tex`k`} vectors
{tex`\mathbf{x_1},\mathbf{x_2},\ldots,\mathbf{x_k}`}:

:::definition[Span]

The _span_ of {tex`k`} vectors is defined
as the set of all linear combinations:

{tex`
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{x_1},\mathbf{x_2},\ldots,\mathbf{x_k})
=\{\alpha_1\mathbf{x_1}
+\alpha_2\mathbf{x_2}
+\cdots
+\alpha_k\mathbf{x_k}
\:|\:\alpha_i\in\mathbb{R} \text{ for } i=1,\ldots,k\}`}

:::

If these vectors {tex`\mathbf{x_i}`} are {tex`n`}-dimensional,
the span is a subspace of {tex`\mathbb{R}^n`}.

## Linear independence

### Case of two vectors

:::question

What is the span
of two parallel vectors {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`}?

:::

If {tex`\mathbf{y}=\lambda\mathbf{x},`} then

{tex`
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
= \mathrm{span}(\mathbf{x})
= \mathrm{span}(\mathbf{y}),`}

so the span is just a line through the origin
containing both vectors.
In that case, we say the vectors {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`}
are _linearly dependent_.
The vector {tex`\mathbf{y}`} does not add a new direction
to the span of {tex`\mathbf{x}`}.

Conversely, the vectors {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`}
are called _linearly independent_
if {tex`\mathbf{y}`} is not in the span of {tex`\mathbf{x}`},
that is, when it actually adds a new direction:
the span then grows from a line to a plane.

:::example[Two independent vectors in ℝ²]

Take the vectors of the left figure above:

{tex`
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
3
\end{bmatrix}`}

If {tex`\mathbf{y}`} were in the span of {tex`\mathbf{x}`},
there would be a scalar {tex`\alpha`} such that
{tex`\mathbf{y}=\alpha\mathbf{x}`},
which is obviously impossible since {tex`\mathbf{y}`} is not
parallel to {tex`\mathbf{x}`}.
So {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} are linearly independent,
and their span is the whole plane {tex`\mathbb{R}^2`}.

:::

:::example[Two dependent vectors in ℝ³]

Consider

{tex`
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
2 \\
-3
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
4 \\
-6
\end{bmatrix}`}

In this case, {tex`\mathbf{y}=2\mathbf{x}`},
so {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} are parallel,
{tex`\mathbf{y}`} is in the span of {tex`\mathbf{x}`}:
we conclude that {tex`\mathbf{x}`} and {tex`\mathbf{y}`} are linearly dependent.

:::

### Case of three vectors

Three vectors {tex`\mathbf{x_1},\mathbf{x_2},\mathbf{x_3}`}
are _linearly dependent_ if (at least) one of them can be expressed
as a linear combination of the others,
for example:

{tex`
\mathbf{x_3} = \alpha\mathbf{x_1}+\beta\mathbf{x_2}`}

This means there is redundancy among the vectors;
geometrically, they do not provide independent directions.
For example, they may lie in the same plane in {tex`\mathbb{R}^3`}.
In other words:

{tex`
\mathbf{x_3} \in \mathrm{span}(\mathbf{x_1},\mathbf{x_2}).`}

it is always possible to express (at least) one of them as a linear combination of the other two.

for example if they are not coplanar.

### General case

:::definition[Linear independence]

In general, {tex`k`} vectors are _linearly dependent_
if at least one of them can be written
as a linear combination of the others.
They are called _linearly independent_ if the subspace

{tex`
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{x_1},\mathbf{x_2},\ldots,\mathbf{x_k})`}

cannot be generated by fewer vectors.

:::

## Basis

:::definition[Basis \& dimension]

A _basis_ is a set of linearly independent vectors that spans the entire space.
In other words,
any vector of the space can be expressed as a linear combination of the basis vectors.

The _dimension_ of the space is the number of vectors in a basis.

:::
